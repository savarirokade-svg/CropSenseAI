### 1.1 Early Blight of Tomato

**Crop:** Tomato
**Disease:** Early Blight
**Causal organism:** *Alternaria solani*
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, stems, fruits
**Source-Title:** Early Blight: *Alternaria solani* — TNAU Agritech Portal, Crop Protection, Horticultural Crops, Vegetables, Tomato
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/tomato_diseases_2.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S35

#### Symptoms
- Brown spots with concentric rings in a bull's-eye pattern and a yellow margin.
- Fruit infection occurs through the calyx or stem attachment.
- Brown concentric rings appear on fruits.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Warm, humid weather (24–29°C), rain and wet conditions.

#### Disease spread and causes
- Survival in infected plant debris.
- Primary spread through infected seed.
- Secondary spread by conidia carried by rain and wind.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Remove and destroy crop debris.
- Follow crop rotation.
- Treat seed with *Trichoderma asperellum* (source-stated; dose not reproduced).

#### Biological management
- Seed treatment with *Trichoderma asperellum*.

#### Chemical management
- The source lists contact/systemic fungicides including iprodione, azoxystrobin, mancozeb, metiram, pyraclostrobin, zineb, azoxystrobin + difenoconazole, metiram + pyraclostrobin and tebuconazole + trifloxystrobin.
- **Iprodione may be restricted or withdrawn in some jurisdictions; verify current Indian registration.**
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- Single-institution source (TNAU); no independent confirmation.
- Iprodione registration is uncertain in some jurisdictions.
- Do not use this record as a substitute for local diagnosis.

### 1.2 Late Blight of Tomato

**Crop:** Tomato
**Disease:** Late Blight
**Causal organism:** *Phytophthora infestans* (Mont.) de Bary
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, stems, fruits
**Source-Title:** Late Blight: *Phytophthora infestans* — TNAU Agritech Portal, Crop Protection, Horticultural Crops, Vegetables, Tomato
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/tomato_diseases_8.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S36, S39, S60
**Additional sources:** S39 — TNAU potato late-blight page (same pathogen; RH >90% cross-check); S60 — ICAR *Indian Horticulture* article (abstract only; cool, humid weather).

#### Symptoms
- Water-soaked black lesions on leaves and stems.
- Lesions expand rapidly and the entire leaf becomes necrotic.
- White sporulation (sporangia and sporangiophores) on leaves.
- Dark brown fruit lesions with soft rot and disintegration.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Cool nights, warm days and extended wet conditions from rain and fog.
- Optimum temperature for sporulation: 18–22°C.
- **High humidity is supported, but the exact threshold is unresolved.** The TNAU tomato page contains a humidity statement that is inconsistent with the TNAU potato page S39 (RH >90%) and with the ICAR article S60 (cool, humid weather). It is treated as a probable typographical error and is **not** presented as established fact.

#### Disease spread and causes
- Survival in infected debris.
- Primary spread from infected debris.
- Secondary spread by wind.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Ensure proper drainage.
- Follow crop rotation.
- Apply *Trichoderma asperellum* to the soil (source-stated; dose not reproduced).

#### Biological management
- Soil application of *Trichoderma asperellum*.

#### Chemical management
- The source lists cyazofamid, azoxystrobin, mancozeb, zineb and azoxystrobin + difenoconazole.
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- **The exact relative-humidity threshold is unresolved.** Do not carry the questionable humidity statement into farmer advice.
- S60 is an abstract only; its full text was not accessed.
- Single-institution tomato source for the detailed symptoms and management.

### 2.1 Late Blight of Potato

**Crop:** Potato
**Disease:** Late Blight
**Causal organism:** *Phytophthora infestans*
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, stems, tubers
**Source-Title:** Late Blight of Potato — TNAU Agritech Portal, Crop Protection, Horticultural Crops, Vegetables, Potato
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/crop_prot_crop%20diseases_veg_potato_1.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S39, S42
**Additional source:** S42 — ICAR ePubs abstract (secondary; abstract only; accessed 2026-10-30).

#### Symptoms
- Water-soaked spots on leaves that enlarge and turn purple-brown, then black.
- White growth develops on the underside of leaves.
- The infection spreads to petioles, rachis and stems, often at nodes; stems may break and plants topple.
- Tubers develop purplish-brown spots that spread over the surface; cut tubers show rusty-brown necrosis from the surface towards the centre.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Relative humidity above 90%.
- Temperature around 10–25°C, with night temperature around 10°C.
- Cloudy conditions before rain and rainfall of at least 0.1 mm, followed by wet days.

#### Disease spread and causes
- Infected tubers and infected soil are the primary infection source.
- Diseased tubers are mainly responsible for carry-over from crop to crop.
- Airborne sporangia cause secondary spread.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Use healthy seed tubers.
- Avoid harvest injuries and do not store visibly infected tubers.
- Destroy foliage a few days before harvest where recommended.
- The source lists resistant varieties: Kufri Naveen, Kufri Jeevan, Kufri Alenkar, Kufri Khasi Garo and Kufri Moti.

#### Biological management
- Not stated on the accessed TNAU page.

#### Chemical management
- The source lists protective sprays of mancozeb or zineb.
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- The ICAR abstract S42 confirms the pathogen and yield importance but not the detailed management.
- No biological management is stated.
- The TNAU page is the full-page primary source; the ICAR abstract is secondary.

### 2.2 Black Scurf of Potato

**Crop:** Potato
**Disease:** Black Scurf
**Causal organism:** *Rhizoctonia solani*
**Affected plant parts:** Tubers, eyes, xylem tissue, stems
**Source-Title:** Black Scurf — Potato — TNAU Agritech Portal, Crop Protection, Horticultural Crops, Vegetables, Potato
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/crop_prot_crop%20diseases_veg_potato_4.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S40, S43
**Additional source:** S43 — ICAR ePubs abstract (secondary; abstract only; accessed 2026-10-30).

#### Symptoms
- Black speck, black speck scab or russet scab on tubers.
- Dark brown colour appears on the eyes at sprouting.
- Affected xylem tissue causes wilting of plants.
- Infected tubers show russeting of the skin.
- Hard dry rot with browning of internal tissue.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Moderately cool, wet weather; temperature around 23°C.

#### Disease spread and causes
- Sclerotia on seed tubers are the principal source of infection for the next crop.
- Mycelium in soil can develop new hyphae when conditions become favourable.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Plant disease-free seed tubers only.
- Plant well-sprouted tubers shallowly where recommended.
- Leave infested land fallow for two years where practical.

#### Biological management
- Not stated on the accessed page.

#### Chemical management
- The source lists mercuric chloride treatment and soil PCNB application.
- **Mercuric chloride is hazardous; PCNB (pentachloronitrobenzene) is listed as banned for agricultural use in India in PAU 2025-26 (S44).** Neither is a current recommendation. Do not use them.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- No biological management.
- The source’s chemical recommendations are hazardous or banned and must not be used.
- The ICAR abstract S43 is secondary and does not provide management detail.

### 2.3 Common Scab of Potato

**Crop:** Potato
**Disease:** Common Scab
**Causal organism:** *Streptomyces scabies*
**Affected plant parts:** Tubers (periderm); foliage is not the primary target
**Source-Title:** Common Scab / Corky Scab — Potato — TNAU Agritech Portal, Crop Protection, Horticultural Crops, Vegetables, Potato
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/crop_prot_crop%20diseases_veg_potato_5.html

**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S41, S43
**Additional source:** S43 — ICAR ePubs abstract (secondary; abstract only; accessed 2026-10-30).

#### Symptoms
- Corkiness of the tuber periderm is characteristic.
- Russet-like lesions extend about 1/4 inch into the tuber surface.
- Tubers are slightly pitted, with light brown to dark brown lesions.
- Affected tissue may attract insects.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- The source states the disease is severe in alkaline soil; application of alkaline fertilizers such as calcium ammonium nitrate should be avoided.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The pathogen persists in soil and infects the crop each year.
- Infected seed tubers are the main source of long-distance spread.
- The pathogen can survive passage through the animal digestive tract and may spread with farmyard manure.
- The source also lists cabbage, carrot, eggplant, onion, radish, spinach and turnip as hosts.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Plant scab-free seed potatoes.
- Avoid alkaline fertilizers in alkaline soils.
- Use green manuring before planting.
- Follow a four- to six-year rotation with alfalfa under irrigated conditions.

#### Biological management
- Not stated on the accessed page.

#### Chemical management
- The source lists mercuric chloride/formaldehyde seed treatment and soil PCNB.
- **Mercuric chloride and formaldehyde are hazardous; PCNB is listed as banned for agricultural use in India in PAU 2025-26 (S44).** These are not current recommendations. Do not use them.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings

### 3.1 Blast of Rice

**Crop:** Rice
**Disease:** Blast
**Causal organism:** *Magnaporthe oryzae* (syn. *Pyricularia oryzae*, *P. grisea*)
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, leaf collar, culm, nodes, neck, panicle branches, glumes
**Source-Title:** Blast — Crop Protection (Rice) — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/rice_diseases/rice_1.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S01, S02
**Additional source:** S02 — TNAU Paddy Crop Protection expert system (conditions and management detail).

#### Symptoms
- Small bluish-green flecks on leaves that enlarge into spindle-shaped spots with a grey centre and dark brown margin.
- Spots coalesce; severely affected nursery or field looks burnt.
- Nodes become black and can break, killing growth above the node.
- Neck lesions are greyish-brown, girdle the neck, and cause the panicle to fall over.
- Infection before the milky stage prevents grain formation; later infection produces poor-quality grain.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Cloudy skies, frequent rain and drizzle, high relative humidity (90% or more) and wet leaves.
- Temperature around 25–28°C.
- High nitrogen levels and upland/high-elevation conditions.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The fungus survives in infected seed and rice stubble.
- Wind carries spores from stubble and from new lesions to other plants over distance.
- Dense growth, high nitrogen and continuous wet foliage increase spread.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Use resistant or tolerant varieties.
- Avoid dry nurseries and late planting.
- Burn straw and stubbles after harvest and remove weed hosts from bunds.
- Treat seed with *Pseudomonas fluorescens* talc formulation and use root dipping/foliar sprays as described by the source.
- Avoid excess nitrogen and apply nitrogen in split doses.

#### Biological management
- *Pseudomonas fluorescens* talc formulation for seed treatment, nursery root dip and foliar spray (source-stated; doses not reproduced).

#### Chemical management
- The source lists seed treatment with captan, carbendazim, thiram or tricyclazole.
- The source lists foliar options including tricyclazole, edifenphos and carbendazim.
- **Edifenphos is a hazardous organophosphate; treat it as requiring expert verification and do not present it as routine advice.**
- No doses or stage-specific spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- Only TNAU sources were used; an independent second source is desirable.
- Variety recommendations are Tamil Nadu-specific and may not transfer to other states.
- Edifenphos is hazardous.

### 3.2 Bacterial Leaf Blight of Rice

**Crop:** Rice
**Disease:** Bacterial Leaf Blight
**Causal organism:** *Xanthomonas oryzae* pv. *oryzae*
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, seedlings (kresek phase), grain
**Source-Title:** Bacterial Leaf Blight — Crop Protection (Rice) — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/rice_diseases/rice_3.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S03, S04
**Additional source:** S04 — TNAU Paddy Crop Protection expert system (favourable factors and management).

#### Symptoms
- Three phases: leaf blight, kresek (seedling wilt) and pale-yellow leaf.
- Seedling infection shows yellow spots at leaf margins that enlarge and dry the foliage.
- Kresek appears 1–2 weeks after transplanting; seedlings wilt and die.
- In older plants, water-soaked lesions start at leaf margins, become wavy, and turn straw-yellow or white.
- Milky bacterial ooze may appear as dew drops on young lesions; cut leaf ends release ooze into water.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Clipping seedling tips at transplanting.
- Heavy rain, heavy dew, flooding and deep irrigation water.
- Severe wind and temperatures of 25–30°C.
- Excessive nitrogen, particularly late top dressing.
- Weeds, infected stubble and ratoons in and around the field.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The bacterium survives in infected stubble, ratoons, paddy water and irrigation canals.
- Spread occurs through irrigation water, rain splash, wind, trimming tools and handling during transplanting.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Use disease-free seed and raise nurseries in isolated upland conditions where possible.
- Avoid clipping seedlings during transplanting.
- Avoid excess nitrogen and skip nitrogen at booting if disease is moderate.
- Drain the field except at flowering, destroy weeds and collateral hosts, and prevent water flow from affected fields.
- Maintain proper plant spacing.
- Grow tolerant varieties listed by the source.

#### Biological management
- The source lists neem oil/NSKE and a fresh cow-dung extract spray as non-chemical options (source-stated; concentrations not reproduced).

#### Chemical management
- The source lists seed-treatment options including bleaching powder plus zinc sulfate and antibiotic formulations. Mercury-based and antibiotic products are not routine recommendations; verify current registration and state restrictions.
- Foliar options include streptomycin sulphate plus tetracycline with copper oxychloride, copper fungicides and streptocyclin.
- **Antibiotic recommendations may be restricted in some states; verify state and CIB&RC guidance before any use.**
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- Only TNAU sources were used.
- Some chemical treatments listed by the source may be dated; local extension advice is required.
- Antibiotic use is regulated; do not treat it as routine advice.

### 3.3 Sheath Blight of Rice

**Crop:** Rice
**Disease:** Sheath Blight
**Causal organism:** *Rhizoctonia solani* (sexual stage *Thanatephorus cucumeris*)
**Affected plant parts:** Leaf sheaths, leaves, tillers
**Source-Title:** Sheath Blight — Crop Protection (Rice) — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/rice_diseases/rice_6.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S05

#### Symptoms
- Initial oval, elliptical or irregular greenish-grey spots on leaf sheaths near the water line.
- Centres become greyish-white with an irregular blackish-brown or purple-brown border.
- Lesions on upper plant parts coalesce and can cover tillers from the water line to the flag leaf.
- Large sheath lesions kill whole leaves; severe infection kills tillers and plants.
- Early heading/grain-filling infection produces poorly filled grain, especially in the lower panicle.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Sclerotia floating on irrigation water or present in soil.
- Relative humidity of 96–100% and temperature of 28–32°C.
- High nitrogen, high seeding rate, close spacing and frequent rain.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The pathogen survives as sclerotia or mycelium in dry soil for about 20 months, and 5–8 months in moist soil.
- Sclerotia spread through irrigation water.
- The fungus has a wide host range.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Use moderately resistant varieties listed by the source.
- Avoid flowing irrigation water from infected fields to healthy fields.
- Deep-plough in summer and burn stubbles.
- Apply organic amendments such as neem cake or farmyard manure as stated by the source.

#### Biological management
- Soil and foliar application of *Bacillus subtilis* (source-stated; doses and exact timing not reproduced).

#### Chemical management
- The source lists a carbendazim spray.
- No dose or spray interval is reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- Only one TNAU source was used.
- Variety and dose recommendations are Tamil Nadu-specific.

### 4.1 Yellow Rust of Wheat

**Crop:** Wheat
**Disease:** Yellow Rust (Stripe Rust)
**Causal organism:** *Puccinia striiformis* f.sp. *tritici*
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, leaf sheaths, necks, glumes
**Source-Title:** Stripe Rust / Yellow Rust — Wheat — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/wheat/crop_prot_crop%20diseases_cereals_wheat_3.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; partial (favourable conditions not stated); PAU S44 supplies conditions and management
**Source IDs:** S06, S44
**Additional source:** S44 — PAU *Package of Practices for Crops of Punjab, Rabi 2025-26* (Punjab-specific conditions, management and varieties).

#### Symptoms
- Yellow to orange-yellow pustules containing urediospores, usually arranged in narrow stripes on leaves.
- Pustules can also appear on leaf sheaths, necks and glumes.
- PAU adds that the disease appears first in sub-mountainous areas and that severe attacks can affect leaf sheaths, awns and glumes.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Minimum temperature of 7–13°C with 85–100% relative humidity at night.
- Maximum daytime temperature of 15–24°C.
- Frequent rain and wind during February–March increase severity and spread.

#### Disease spread and causes
- TNAU: the pathogen survives on stubbles and volunteer crops; the alternate host is recorded as unknown; urediospores from hill areas spread the disease.
- PAU: the disease appears first in sub-mountainous areas and spreads with wind and rain.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Grow rust-resistant varieties. PAU lists PBW Biscuit 1, PBW Zinc 2, PBW RS1, PBW 725, Unnat PBW 550, PBW 752, WHD 943, PDW 291 and PBW 660.
- Do not grow unrecommended varieties, especially in the sub-mountainous zone.
- Do not sow wheat in October in disease-prone sub-mountainous areas.
- Monitor the crop from the second week of December, especially after irrigation or rain.
- TNAU also lists mixed cropping, crop rotation and avoiding excess nitrogen.

#### Biological management
- Not stated in the accessed sources.

#### Chemical management
- TNAU lists sulphur dusting and mancozeb.
- PAU lists captan + hexaconazole, tebuconazole, trifloxystrobin + tebuconazole, azoxystrobin + cyproconazole, pyraclostrobin + epoxiconazole, azoxystrobin + tebuconazole and propiconazole.
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- No biological management is stated.
- PAU is Punjab-specific; variety recommendations may not transfer to other states.
- The two sources do not give the same level of detail; PAU is the main source for Indian conditions and management.

### 4.3 Loose Smut of Wheat

**Crop:** Wheat
**Disease:** Loose Smut
**Causal organism:** *Ustilago segetum* var. *tritici* (*U. tritici*)
**Affected plant parts:** Inflorescence (ear head), except the rachis
**Source-Title:** Loose Smut — Wheat — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/wheat/crop_prot_crop%20diseases_cereals_wheat_4.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; partial (spread/conditions not stated); S44/S52/S57 supply conditions and management
**Source IDs:** S08, S44, S52, S57
**Additional sources:** S44 — PAU package (Punjab management); S52 — JNKVV loose-smut review (epidemiology); S57 — ICAR-IIWBR pocket guide (integrated management).

#### Symptoms
- The entire inflorescence except the rachis is replaced by masses of black smut spores.
- Teliospores are blown away by wind, leaving a bare rachis and remnants of floral structures.
- S52 adds that diseased ears emerge from the boot leaf a little earlier than healthy ears, and the blossoms are replaced by a black powdery mass of teliospores.
- In many varieties, only a few tillers or part of the ear may be smutted.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Infection is most likely in cool, humid weather with light showers or heavy dews (S52).
- The source records 23°C and 60–85% relative humidity as favourable.
- Infection occurs during flowering; the amount of disease varies from year to year with weather at flowering.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The disease is internally seed-borne; the pathogen survives as secondary mycelium in the embryo.
- At flowering, teliospores are dispersed by wind and land on healthy flowers; they germinate and the fungus becomes dormant in the ovary/embryo.
- The infected seed carries the fungus to the next crop, where it grows systemically inside the plant.
- S52 notes that a field with only 1% infected heads can produce seed with at least 10% loose smut infection when flowering weather is favourable.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Use clean, healthy seed from a disease-free source.
- Grow resistant varieties where recommended. S52 lists Kalyan, DWR-59, NP-120 and Pb-90; these are source-specific and should be checked locally.
- Bury infected ear heads in soil to avoid secondary spread.
- Use hot-water seed treatment or solar seed treatment where recommended.
- Use only systemic seed-treatment fungicides; contact fungicides are not effective against loose smut (S52).
- ICAR-IIWBR's integrated approach combines a reduced chemical seed treatment with *Trichoderma viride*; exact timing is not reproduced.

#### Biological management
- *Trichoderma viride* seed treatment is part of ICAR-IIWBR's integrated loose-smut module (source-stated; dose not reproduced).

#### Chemical management
- TNAU lists carboxin (Vitavax).
- PAU lists tebuconazole, carboxin + tetramethyl thiuram disulphide, or carboxin seed treatments.
- ICAR-IIWBR lists carboxin, carbendazim, tebuconazole or a reduced-carboxin integrated option combined with *T. viride*.
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- Resistant-variety names are region-specific; verify local recommendations.
- The 1% head infection to 10% seed infection example is a source-reported field observation, not a general threshold.
- The exact timing of the integrated *Trichoderma* + fungicide approach is not reproduced.

### 6.1 Bacterial Blight of Cotton

**Crop:** Cotton
**Disease:** Bacterial Blight
**Causal organism:** *Xanthomonas campestris* pv. *malvacearum*
**Affected plant parts:** Cotyledons, leaves, veins, stems, fruiting branches, bolls, seed
**Source-Title:** Bacterial Blight — Cotton — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/cotton_diseases/cotton_d9.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S12

#### Symptoms
- **Seedling blight:** small, water-soaked, circular or irregular cotyledon lesions; infection spreads through the petiole to the stem and kills seedlings.
- **Angular leaf spot:** small, dark green, water-soaked spots on the lower leaf surface; spots become angular, turn reddish-brown and spread along veins.
- **Vein necrosis / vein banding:** veins and veinlets blacken, giving a blighted appearance; bacterial ooze forms crusts/scales; leaves crinkle and wither.
- **Blackarm:** dark brown to black lesions on stems and fruiting branches, girdling them; stem cracking and gummosis; dead branches hang like a black arm.
- **Square/boll rot:** water-soaked boll lesions turn dark black and sunken; bolls shed or burst prematurely; lint is stained and seed quality reduced.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Optimum soil temperature around 28°C and air temperature of 30–40°C.
- Relative humidity around 85%.
- Poor tillage, late irrigation, potassium deficiency.
- Rain followed by bright sunshine, especially in October–November.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The bacterium survives on infected dried plant debris in soil for several years.
- It is seed-borne in the slimy mass on seed fuzz.
- Primary infection is seed-borne; secondary spread is by wind, rain splash, irrigation water, insects and implements.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Delint seed and treat it before sowing.
- Remove and destroy infected plant debris.
- Rogue out volunteer cotton plants and weed hosts.
- Follow crop rotation with non-host crops.
- Early thinning and early earthing-up with potash.

#### Biological management
- Not stated on the accessed page.

#### Chemical management
- The source lists seed treatment with carboxin/oxycarboxin or carboxin + thiram.
- The source lists a foliar spray of streptomycin sulphate + copper oxychloride.
- **Antibiotic use in agriculture is restricted in some Indian states; verify state and CIB&RC guidance before any use.**
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- Single-source record; no biological management stated.
- Antibiotic use is regulated; do not treat it as routine advice.
- The TNAU cotton index has a separate mislabeled “Anthracnose” link (S15) that is actually *Alternaria* leaf blight; it is not used here.

### 7.1 Yellow Mosaic of Soybean

**Crop:** Soybean
**Disease:** Yellow Mosaic
**Causal organism:** *Mungbean yellow mosaic India virus* (MYMIV) in Central and northern India and *Mungbean yellow mosaic virus* (MYMV) in South India. S67 works on MYMIV in Central India.
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves
**Source-Title:** Improved Technologies and Recommendations for Maximizing Soybean Productivity (Extension Bulletin No. 18, 2023) — ICAR-Indian Institute of Soybean Research
**Source-URL:** https://icar-nsri.res.in/pdfdoc/ExtensionBulletin2023E_2.pdf
**Source-Institution:** ICAR-Indian Institute of Soybean Research (ICAR-IISR), Indore
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full PDF read; relevant disease chapter verified
**Source IDs:** S16, S46, S67
**Additional sources:** S16 — TNAU soybean yellow mosaic page (symptoms/roguing); S67 — JNKVV weather/whitefly study, Jabalpur (location-specific weather and vector data).

#### Symptoms
- Small yellow patches or spots appear on young leaves.
- New trifoliate leaves show irregular yellow and green patches alternating with each other.
- Yellow discoloration slowly increases; newly formed leaves may turn completely yellow.
- Infected leaves show severe mottling and crinkling.
- Infected plants mature late and bear few flowers and pods; pods are small and distorted.
- Early infection can kill the plant before seed set; oil content is reduced.
- TNAU adds that rusty necrotic spots appear in yellow areas as leaves mature.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- **Vector conditions:** S67 states that whitefly requires a temperature higher than 26°C and relative humidity above 60% for optimum development (citing Nene 1972; Butler et al. 1983). This is a vector condition, not a disease threshold.
- **Jabalpur field observations (kharif 2021):** YMD initiation occurred at maximum 33.3°C, minimum 25.1°C, rainfall 33.2 mm, morning RH 85.1% and evening RH 58.3%, with a high whitefly population. Maximum disease progression occurred at maximum 28.5°C, minimum 24.2°C, morning RH 90.0% and evening RH 79.0%.
- S67 concludes that high temperature (minimum 25.0°C to maximum 33.5°C), low rainfall and relatively less humid conditions (60–85%) combined with a high whitefly population may lead to severe spread in the upcoming fortnight.
- These are **location- and season-specific observations**, not a universal prediction model. The IISR bulletin S46 notes that incidence is lower in timely-sown crops and that whitefly activity drives spread.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The virus is spread by the whitefly *Bemisia tabaci*.
- **Transmission-mode conflict:** S46 describes transmission as **semi-persistent**; S67 describes it as **persistent**. The sources disagree. This guide does not assert a definitive mode; the conflict is unresolved. Both sources agree that *B. tabaci* is the vector.
- The virus has a wide host range, including pulses and weeds.
- TNAU adds that roguing infected plants up to 30 days after sowing is recommended.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Grow yellow-mosaic-resistant/tolerant varieties. The IISR bulletin lists several, including NRC 142, NRC 127, MACS 1407, MACS 1520, MACS 1460, JS 20-116, JS 20-94, JS 20-98, RSC 10-46, RSC 10-52 and AMS-MB-5-18.
- Treat seed with thiamethoxam or imidacloprid where yellow mosaic and stem fly occur every year.
- Intercrop with maize.
- Avoid synthetic pyrethroids, which can cause whitefly resurgence.
- Rogue out infected plants early (TNAU).
- Use yellow sticky traps and a neem kernel extract spray as part of the integrated programme.

#### Biological management
- IISR lists neem kernel extract as part of the integrated whitefly/YMV programme (source-stated; concentration and timing not reproduced).

#### Chemical management
- IISR lists thiamethoxam or imidacloprid seed treatment and foliar options; the source's timing is not reproduced.
- TNAU lists a methyldemeton spray.
- **Methyldemeton is a highly hazardous organophosphate; it is not recommended. Use the IISR integrated whitefly/YMV programme instead.**
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- **Transmission mode is unresolved:** S46 says semi-persistent; S67 says persistent. Do not state one as definitive.
- The S67 weather range is from one location and season (Jabalpur, kharif 2021); it is not a universal threshold.
- Whitefly population dynamics are not quantified in the accessed sources.
- Methyldemeton is hazardous and rejected.

- Blackening and cracking of roots is the most common symptom.

### 8.3 Rust of Groundnut

**Crop:** Groundnut
**Disease:** Rust
**Causal organism:** *Puccinia arachidis*
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, stems, petioles; all above-ground parts except flowers
**Source-Title:** Rust — Groundnut — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/groundnut_diseases/groundnut_d3.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S19, S20, S47, S59
**Additional sources:** S19 — TNAU organic-farming page; S47 — ICAR-IIGR bulletin; S59 — current ICAR-IIGR Crop Protection page (location-specific propiconazole result).

#### Symptoms
- Initially chlorotic spots develop on the upper leaf surface.
- Orange-coloured pustules (uredinia) appear on the lower surface and later turn brown.
- Pustules are about 0.5–1.4 mm in diameter.
- Severely infected leaves turn necrotic and desiccate but remain attached to the plant.
- Symptoms appear on above-ground parts except flowers; kernels are shriveled and small.
- TNAU adds that the disease usually appears after the crop is six weeks old and that leaf spots and rust can occur together.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Temperature around 20°C.
- Prolonged leaf-wetness hours.
- High humidity.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The pathogen survives as urediniospores and on self-sown (volunteer) groundnut plants infected by rust.
- Spread within crops is facilitated by wind, rain splash and insects.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Destroy volunteer (self-sown) groundnut plants.
- Sow early (first fortnight of June) to avoid the disease.
- Intercrop pearl millet or sorghum with groundnut in a 1:3 row ratio.
- Use resistant/tolerant varieties.
- TNAU also lists removing diseased plants, controlling weeds and avoiding continuous monocropping.

#### Biological management
- ICAR-IIGR S47 lists aqueous neem leaf extract or neem seed kernel extract as non-chemical options (source-stated; concentrations not reproduced).
- The current ICAR-IIGR page S59 lists *Trichoderma* and *Pseudomonas* for soil-borne diseases (collar rot, stem rot, root rot and pod rot). These are **not** rust recommendations; do not present them as rust control.

#### Chemical management
The sources do **not** agree on a single rust chemical recommendation:

- **TNAU S20 (2015):** mancozeb, chlorothalonil, wettable sulphur or tridemorph; the source's repeat interval is not reproduced.
- **ICAR-IIGR S47 (2015):** mancozeb, chlorothalonil or “tridimefon” (probably triadimefon; spelling not confirmed); neem leaf extract/NSKE as non-chemical options.
- **ICAR-IIGR S59 (current page):** propiconazole (spelled “Proficonazole”) found most effective and economical for late leaf spot and rust in rabi/summer at Vriddhachalam — a **location-specific** result.

**Where they agree:** mancozeb and chlorothalonil appear in both TNAU S20 and ICAR-IIGR S47; neem is listed by S19/S47; the cultural practices are broadly consistent.

**Where they differ:** TNAU adds wettable sulphur and tridemorph; ICAR-IIGR S47 adds triadimefon and neem; the current ICAR-IIGR page adds propiconazole at one location. The doses and intervals also differ.

**Why they differ:** different publication dates (TNAU 2015; S47 2015; S59 current), different locations (Tamil Nadu; national/Gujarat; Vriddhachalam) and different scopes (crop-protection page; integrated bulletin; location-specific research result).

**Safety:**
- **Tridemorph is listed as banned for agricultural use in India in PAU 2025-26 (S44); do not use it.**
- “Tridimefon” in S47 is probably triadimefon; the spelling is not confirmed. Verify the active ingredient and current registration before any use.
- Propiconazole is source-listed for a specific location; its concentration is not reproduced here. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced.

**Conclusion:** no single harmonised recommendation is supported by the accessed evidence. The common cultural/biological options can be presented; chemical choice remains location-specific and label-specific. Do not merge the three chemical lists into one universal recommendation.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- The TNAU and ICAR-IIGR recommendations overlap but are not identical; the difference is documented, not resolved.
- The current ICAR-IIGR page gives a location-specific propiconazole result, not a national rust recommendation.
- The ICAR-IIGR 2015 bulletin's “tridimefon” spelling and identity need confirmation.
- No source gives a single nationally validated rust chemical schedule.

**Crop:** Mustard
**Disease:** Sclerotinia Stem Rot
**Causal organism:** *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*

**Source-Title:** Sclerotinia Stem Rot — Rapeseed and Mustard — TNAU Agritech Portal

**Source IDs:** S26, S44, S45
**Additional sources:** S44 — PAU package (Indian conditions and management); S45 — ICAR-DRMR (biological/chemical management).

#### Symptoms
- Water-soaked stem spots that may later be covered with cottony white growth.
- Affected stem portions become bleached and eventually shred.
- Girdling causes premature ripening and lodging.
- Hard black sclerotia form inside the stem and occasionally on the surface.
- Basal stalk infections are rare.
- PAU adds that affected plants look wilted, then dry up and stand erect with a whitish appearance; heavy canopies may break at the stem.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- The disease generally appears in January when temperature is low and soil moisture is abundant (PAU).
- Sclerotia survive in soil for long periods.

#### Disease spread and causes
- Sclerotia in soil and crop debris are the carry-over source; they germinate and the mycelium enters plants.
- The page does not describe long-distance spread in detail.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Use crop rotation and do not plant highly susceptible crops more than once in four years.
- Use at least a five-year rotation for severely infested fields.
- Avoid planting next to a field with severe Sclerotinia in the previous four to five years.
- Control broad-leaved weeds.
- Plant thoroughly cleaned seed and avoid dense stands.

### 11.2 Leaf Blight of Onion

**Crop:** Onion
**Disease:** Leaf Blight (Botrytis)
**Causal organism:** *Botrytis* spp. (Indian evidence). UC ANR identifies *Botrytis squamosa* as the leaf-blight/blast pathogen (international evidence). **The exact species in India is probable, not confirmed.**
**Affected plant parts:** Foliage; bulbs can rot and are a source of airborne spores
**Source-Title:** Leaf Blight (Botrytis) — Onion — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/onion_diseases_3.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; partial (species/conditions/spread from S53)
**Source IDs:** S29, S53
**Additional source:** S53 — UC ANR Botrytis leaf blight page (non-Indian secondary source; species identity and spread).

#### Indian versus international evidence
- **Indian evidence:** TNAU S29 identifies the pathogen only as *Botrytis* spp. No Indian source retrieved confirms *B. squamosa*.
- **International evidence:** UC ANR S53 identifies *B. squamosa* and describes its symptoms, airborne spores, leaf-wetness requirement and sclerotia.
- **Conclusion:** *B. squamosa* is a probable identity for the Indian disease, not a confirmed one. This guide does not claim that *B. squamosa* is confirmed in India. Do not upgrade the species name without Indian confirmation.

#### Symptoms
- TNAU: the disease is major in cool-climate onion areas; light infections may not affect yield, but heavy infections can cause major loss.
- Hundreds of white specks appear on the foliage; the disease spreads rapidly and can kill the tops of the entire crop.
- UC ANR: white, sunken, oval spots 0.5–6 mm long, sometimes with a light-green halo; leaf tips die back and whole leaves may die.
- Severely affected fields appear blighted; premature leaf death reduces bulb size.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- Cool climate (TNAU).
- UC ANR: leaf surfaces must be wet from dew or rain for 20 or more hours for leaf spots to develop.
- Optimal temperature for spore germination is about 15°C; optimal mycelial growth is in the mid-20s°C.
- Poor air circulation in the canopy favours the disease.

#### Disease spread and causes
- UC ANR: *B. squamosa* spores are airborne and land on leaf surfaces; in the presence of moisture they germinate and kill leaf tissue.
- The fungus forms sclerotia on infected tissue that can persist in soil for months to years.
- Bulbs are a major source of airborne spores, so seed fields should be isolated from fresh-market/processing fields.
- These spread details are from the non-Indian source and should not be presented as Indian-specific epidemiology.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Treat bulbs with captan or thiram (TNAU).
- UC ANR: isolate seed fields from fresh-market/processing onion fields.
- Rotate away from *Allium* crops for three years to reduce soil sclerotia.
- Destroy volunteer and cull onions during the rotation.
- Plant with single-row spacing of at least 12 inches to speed leaf drying.
- Time sprinkler irrigation to avoid extended leaf wetness.
- These UC ANR practices are not Indian state recommendations.

#### Biological management
- Not stated in the accessed sources.

#### Chemical management
- TNAU lists bulb treatment with captan/thiram and sprays of maneb, mancozeb or chlorothalonil.
- UC ANR lists several fungicide groups (SDHI/QoI, fluazinam, chlorothalonil, penthiopyrad), but these are US registrations and are **not** Indian recommendations.
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- **Indian confirmation of the exact species is unavailable.** The *B. squamosa* identification and detailed spread come from a non-Indian source.
- No Indian cultural-management package was retrieved.
- This is **not** a Stemphylium blight source; do not relabel it as one.
- Do not present the UC ANR fungicide groups as Indian recommendations.

### 11.3 Basal Rot of Onion

**Crop:** Onion
**Disease:** Basal Rot
**Causal organism:** *Fusarium oxysporum* f.sp. *cepae*
**Affected plant parts:** Leaves, bulb, roots, basal plate
**Source-Title:** Basal Rot — Onion — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/onion_diseases_1.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; partial (epidemiology from S50; biocontrol from S66)
**Source IDs:** S30, S50, S66
**Additional sources:** S50 — TNAU post-harvest basal-rot page (epidemiology and spread); S66 — TNAU Madurai field trial (experimental biocontrol).

#### Symptoms
- Leaves turn yellow and then dry slowly.
- Affected plants show drying of the leaf tip downwards.
- The entire plant may show complete drying of the foliage.
- The bulb shows soft rotting and the roots rot.
- A whitish mouldy growth appears on the scale.
- The disease can begin in the field and continue in storage.
- S50 adds that the disease occurs in patches.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- High temperature and low soil moisture favour high disease incidence (S50).
- The recorded favourable temperature range is 28–32°C.
- The source notes that onions are sensitive to low soil copper levels.

#### Disease spread and causes
- The pathogen is soil-borne and survives as thick-walled chlamydospores.
- Infection occurs through the root, either directly or through wounds.
- Soil insects are common carriers of the pathogen.
- The pathogen persists in the field and can continue in storage.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Follow crop rotation.
- Destroy infected plant debris (field sanitation).
- Cure harvested bulbs thoroughly to reduce storage losses.
- Check and correct soil copper levels where deficient.
- Grow resistant varieties where locally recommended; S50 lists Bellary, Poona Red Globe, Patna Red and White Large.

#### Biological management (experimental)
- A TNAU field trial in Madurai, Tamil Nadu, tested *Trichoderma harzianum* TH3, *Pseudomonas* isolates Pf12 and Pf27, and their combinations (S66).
- The three-agent combination (Pf12 + Pf27 + TH3) reduced basal rot by 85% in the field; individual agents reduced disease by about 77–79%; carbendazim (the chemical comparator) reduced it by 88%.
- **This is an experimental field result, not a state extension recommendation.** The formulation, dose, application method and local adaptation were trial-specific and are not reproduced here. Do not present it as an established farmer recommendation.
- The trial used carbendazim as a comparator, not benomyl; carbendazim itself still requires current registration and label verification.

#### Chemical management
- S30 lists a soil drench with copper oxychloride.
- S50 lists a pre-planting bulb treatment with benomyl + mancozeb. **Benomyl is banned for agricultural use in India (PAU 2025-26 banned list, S44); this recommendation is not usable.**
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- Biological control is experimental, not an extension recommendation; no state-level biological package was retrieved.
- The benomyl recommendation is obsolete and must not be carried into farmer advice.
- The S66 trial is location-specific and used experimental formulations; its results should not be generalised without local validation.

### 12.1 Anthracnose of Chilli

**Crop:** Chilli
**Disease:** Anthracnose (Fruit Rot and Die Back)
**Causal organism:** *Colletotrichum capsici* is the name used by TNAU S31, PAU S48 and the Indian weather study S62. Chilli anthracnose in India is a **species complex**; S63 reports *C. capsici*, *C. acutatum* and *C. gloeosporioides* from India, with *C. capsici* causing major damage at the ripe-fruit stage. The review's table also lists *C. truncatum* (*capsici*), reflecting taxonomic ambiguity. This record focuses on *C. capsici* but does not imply it is the only species.
**Affected plant parts:** Tender twigs, flowers, flower stalks, stems, branches, fruits
**Source-Title:** Fruit Rot and Die Back — Chilli — TNAU Agritech Portal
**Source-URL:** https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_protection/chilli_diseases_2.html
**Source-Institution:** Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Agritech Portal
**Source-Access-Date:** 2026-11-03
**Source-Access-Status:** Full page read; verified
**Source IDs:** S31, S48, S49, S62, S63
**Additional sources:** S48 — PAU vegetable package (Punjab management); S49 — ICAR-NRIIPM IPM module (TLS warning; integrated management only); S62 — Kanpur anthracnose weather study; S63 — BHU chilli-anthracnose review.

#### Symptoms
- The fungus causes necrosis of tender twigs from the tip backwards, hence “die-back.”
- Infection usually begins when the crop is in flower.
- Flowers drop and dry up; there is profuse flower shedding.
- The flower stalk shrivels and dries; drying spreads to the stem and causes die-back of branches.
- Partially affected plants bear few, low-quality fruits.
- Necrotic areas are separated from healthy tissue by a dark brown to black band.
- PAU adds that dark sunken fruit spots are covered with dark or pinkish fruiting bodies, often arranged in concentric rings.
- S63 adds that fruit lesions are sunken and circular or angular; multiple lesions can coalesce; black spots in concentric rings and orange-to-pink conidial masses may be visible.

#### Favourable environmental conditions
- S62 recorded anthracnose at Kanpur in 2021–22 and 2022–23:
  - 2021–22: disease onset in the 45th meteorological week (November) at maximum 28.8°C, minimum 12.8°C, RH 67.5% and no rainfall; peak disease in the 13th week (March 2022) at maximum 38.2°C, minimum 18.4°C and RH 50.1%.
  - 2022–23: onset in the 45th week at maximum 29.9°C, minimum 16.3°C and RH 94.0%; peak in the 13th week (March 2023) at maximum 32.2°C, minimum 16.7°C, RH 82.0% and 39.2 mm rainfall.
  - The maximum infection rate occurred in the 46th–47th weeks of November in both seasons.
- These are **location- and season-specific field observations**, not a universal temperature or humidity threshold.
- S63 states that hot, humid conditions favour the disease; it cites 27°C and RH 80% as the most favourable conditions reported in the literature, and emphasises leaf-surface wetness, rainfall intensity/duration and light. This is a review statement, not an Indian field threshold.

#### Disease spread and causes
- S63: the pathogen is **seed-borne, soil-borne, water-borne and airborne**.
- Rain splash can spread conidia from infected to healthy plant parts; leaf wetness is directly linked to severity.
- The pathogen survives as microsclerotia in seed, soil and plant debris.
- S62 notes that rainfall and wind can affect dispersal but does not quantify the mechanism.
- TNAU/PAU's emphasis on disease-free seed and seed treatment is consistent with seed-borne inoculum.

#### Prevention and cultural management
- Use disease-free healthy seed.
- Treat seed with thiram or captan before sowing (doses not reproduced).
- ICAR-NRIIPM's IPM module includes *Trichoderma asperellum* seed treatment, raised nursery beds, nylon netting, destruction of previous crop residues and removal of infected plants.
- S63 adds: use transplants raised from disease-free seed; keep the nursery weed-free and away from other solanaceous crops; improve drainage; maintain plant spacing to reduce a dense, humid canopy; rotate for 2–3 years with non-hosts; remove infected plant parts.
- S63 notes that rice-straw and plastic mulches have been reported as cultural options; these are review-based and not Indian state recommendations.

#### Biological management
- ICAR-NRIIPM's IPM module includes *Trichoderma asperellum* seed treatment and soil application enriched with FYM, neem cake and neem oil/azadirachtin sprays.
- S63 lists *Trichoderma viride*, *T. harzianum*, *Pseudomonas fluorescens* and *Bacillus subtilis* as experimental biocontrol agents against *Colletotrichum*; these are experimental, not established farmer recommendations.

#### Chemical management
- TNAU lists ziram, captan, miltox, wettable sulphur, copper oxychloride or zineb.
- PAU lists mancozeb or copper oxychloride.
- S63 lists carbendazim, mancozeb, propiconazole, difenoconazole and azoxystrobin among fungicides tested or used; these are source-listed, not a recommendation.
- No doses or spray schedules are reproduced. Confirm current CIB&RC registration and the local label.

#### Evidence limitations and safety warnings
- The S62 weather values are location- and season-specific; there is no validated universal threshold.
- Chilli anthracnose is a species complex; the exact species mix may vary by region.
- Biological control remains experimental in S63.
- PAU recommendations are Punjab-specific.
- The source's spray intervals are not reproduced.

### G.1 Information to Collect Before Seeking Diagnosis Help

> **Source note:** No specific publication was identified for this checklist. This is general guidance compiled for CropSense AI.

When reporting a problem to CropSense AI, your local KVK, or an agronomist, the following information helps with accurate diagnosis:

1. **Crop and variety:** Which crop is affected (tomato, potato, rice, wheat, maize, cotton, soybean, groundnut, chickpea, mustard, onion or chilli)? What variety are you growing?
2. **Crop age and growth stage:** Seedling, vegetative, flowering, fruiting, or tuber-bulking?
3. **Which part is affected:** Leaves (lower, upper, or all)? Stems? Fruits or tubers? Roots?
4. **Symptom description:** What do the spots, lesions, or other signs look like? Colour, size, shape? Rings, halos, mould, slime, smell?
5. **How widespread:** A few plants, a patch, or the whole field? Is it spreading?
6. **Recent weather:** Heavy rain, high humidity, fog, or unusual temperatures?
7. **Irrigation method:** Overhead sprinkler, drip, or furrow?
8. **Field history:** Has this occurred before? What crops were grown previously?
9. **Recent inputs:** Any fertiliser, pesticide, or soil amendment applied recently?
10. **Photos:** A close-up photograph of the affected part helps significantly with identification.

### G.2 Integrated Pest Management Principles

> **Source note:** No specific publication was identified for this section. This is general guidance.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is an approach that combines multiple methods to manage crop pests and diseases while reducing unnecessary use of chemical pesticides.

**Core IPM principles:**

- **Prevention first:** Healthy soil, clean seed, and good crop management reduce vulnerability to disease.
- **Regular monitoring:** Walk through your field and observe plants carefully — at least once or twice a week. Early detection makes management much easier.
- **Correct identification before action:** Correctly identify the problem before applying any pesticide. Spraying the wrong product wastes money and can harm beneficial organisms.
- **Use the full IPM toolkit:** Cultural practices (crop rotation, sanitation, resistant varieties), biological control, and targeted chemical use when needed.
- **Chemical pesticides as a last resort:** Apply pesticides only when disease pressure warrants the cost and risk. Choose targeted options over broad-spectrum products.
- **Rotate chemical classes:** Avoid applying the same fungicide class repeatedly. Alternate between different modes of action to reduce resistance development.

### G.3 When to Contact a KVK or Agricultural Expert

> **Source note:** No specific publication was identified. General advisory guidance.

Always consult a Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK), your State Agriculture Department, or a licensed agronomist in the following situations:
